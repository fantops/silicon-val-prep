What "switch pre-si validation" actually means

Broadcom's switch silicon (Tomahawk / Trident / Jericho families) are large Ethernet switch ASICs: many high-speed SerDes lanes, MAC/PHY blocks, a packet-processing pipeline, packet buffers/memories, and a control/management subsystem. Pre-silicon validation = verifying the RTL/design before tape-out, in simulation and emulation, against the spec. So the interview leans on RTL-adjacent fundamentals, not board/lab debug.

Why they asked for electronics + digital + CDC specifically A multi-clock networking ASIC lives and dies on clock-domain crossings (SerDes recovered clock ↔ core clock ↔ MAC ↔ management bus). The single biggest class of functional bugs and the hardest to verify is CDC. They want to know you understand the silicon from the transistor up to the synchronizer up to the packet pipeline.

0b.1 ★ "How was demotion decided?" — the PM gap, closed

Demotion = the hardware/firmware choosing a shallower low-power state than the OS requested, because the predicted idle won't pay back the deeper state's entry/exit cost. This is the exact PM question I want to answer crisply — it's my Intel C-state/P-state territory.

Why demotion exists: every deeper idle state (C-state) saves more power but costs more entry + exit latency and flushes more context (caches, voltage ramp). If the CPU wakes too soon, you paid the exit cost for no benefit — and hurt latency. So the platform demotes: OS asks for C6, hardware grants C1/C3 instead.

The decision inputs (what gets weighed):

  • Predicted residency — how long the core is likely to stay idle, estimated from recent idle history (a moving prediction).
  • Break-even / target residency — each C-state's minimum idle time to recoup its entry+exit energy. Stay shorter than this → net loss.
  • Exit-latency tolerance — QoS/latency constraints (e.g. an interrupt or I/O deadline) cap how deep you can go.
  • Recent wake pattern — frequent short wakes ⇒ demote (don't keep paying deep-exit cost); long stable idle ⇒ allow/promote.

The decision flow (the scaffold to narrate):

OS requests C-state X (via MWAIT hint / governor)
        │
   predict idle duration  ── from idle-history
        │
   compare to X's target residency & exit-latency limit
        │
 ┌──────┴───────────────────────────────┐
 predicted idle ≥ break-even          predicted idle < break-even
 & latency OK                          OR latency too tight
        │                                      │
   grant X (or PROMOTE deeper)           DEMOTE → grant a shallower state
        │                                      │
   monitor actual residency ── if we keep mis-predicting, bias future demotion
If asked deeper (P-state vs C-state demotion) C-state demotion = idle-state depth (latency/energy break-even, above). P-state = active voltage/frequency operating point (DVFS): demotion there means not jumping to the highest frequency for a short burst because the V²·f energy + transition cost isn't worth it. Both are the same idea — don't pay a transition cost you won't earn back. honesty my Intel exposure to this was on Zebu pre-si flows (C-state entry/exit handshakes), not real voltage/frequency measurement.

1 · Electronics fundamentals

1.1 Passive basics you must be reflexive on

ConceptKey relationWhy it matters in an ASIC
Ohm's lawV = I·RIR drop on power grid → local Vdd sag → timing failures
CapacitorI = C·dV/dt; charge time ∝ RCGate/wire cap dominates switching delay & dynamic power
RC delayτ = RC, ~63% in 1τ, settle ~5τInterconnect delay; why long nets need buffering/repeaters
Dynamic powerP = α·C·V²·fV² is why DVFS lowers voltage first; α = activity factor
Static/leakageP = V·I_leakDominant at advanced nodes; why power gating exists
Probe-then-push ready "Why does lowering voltage save more power than lowering frequency?" → Dynamic power scales linearly with f but with the square of V. And max f itself depends on V (lower V → slower transistors), so DVFS drops V and f together — a roughly cubic effective benefit. (This is the Intel P&P answer I missed in April — now locked.)

1.2 The MOSFET in one breath

  • NMOS: conducts when gate is high (passes a strong 0, weak 1). Good pull-down.
  • PMOS: conducts when gate is low (passes a strong 1, weak 0). Good pull-up.
  • Three regions: cut-off (VGS<Vth, off), triode/linear (acts like a resistor), saturation (acts like a current source — used in analog/SerDes).
  • Switching speed ∝ drive current / load cap. More width W → more current → faster but more area & cap.

1.3 Logic levels & noise margin

A gate guarantees outputs in VOL/VOH bands and accepts inputs in VIL/VIH bands.

NM_high = V_OH - V_IH     (high-side noise margin)
NM_low  = V_IL - V_OL     (low-side noise margin)

Bigger noise margin → more tolerant of crosstalk, IR drop, SI noise. On a switch ASIC with thousands of fast lanes, signal integrity (crosstalk, ISI, reflections) is a first-class concern — that's why SerDes use equalization (CTLE/DFE) and differential signaling.

2 · CMOS & logic gates

2.1 The CMOS inverter — the canonical question

PMOS on top (to Vdd), NMOS on bottom (to GND), gates tied together as input, drains tied as output.

InputPMOSNMOSOutput
0ONOFF1 (pulled to Vdd)
1OFFON0 (pulled to GND)
Why CMOS won In steady state, exactly one transistor is off, so there's no DC path from Vdd to GND → near-zero static power. Power is burned only during switching (charging C, plus brief short-circuit current when both are momentarily on). That's the whole α·C·V²·f story.

2.2 Pull-up / pull-down network rule

  • PUN (PMOS) and PDN (NMOS) are duals: series in one ↔ parallel in the other.
  • NAND: NMOS in series (pull-down only when all inputs high), PMOS in parallel.
  • NOR: NMOS in parallel, PMOS in series.
  • NAND is preferred in CMOS (series NMOS beats series PMOS because electrons > holes mobility).

2.3 Universal gates & building blocks

  • NAND / NOR are universal — any logic from them.
  • XOR = inequality / parity / adder sum bit.
  • MUX = select; also universal; basis of LUTs in FPGAs.
  • Half adder: S = A⊕B, C = A·B.
  • Full adder: S = A⊕B⊕Cin, Cout = AB + Cin(A⊕B).
  • Tri-state buffer → enables shared buses.

2.4 Propagation delay, fan-out, glitches

  • tpd = input change → output settle. Critical path = longest combinational tpd between registers → sets max frequency.
  • Fan-out: more loads → more cap → slower. Fix with buffering / gate sizing.
  • Glitch/hazard: transient wrong output from unequal path delays. Harmless inside pure combinational logic that's later registered; dangerous if it feeds an async input, clock, or reset. (Direct CDC relevance — §9.)

3 · Combinational design

  • Combinational = output is a pure function of current inputs, no memory. Adders, MUXes, decoders, comparators, ALUs, priority encoders.
  • Design concerns: minimize logic depth (critical path), avoid glitches on sensitive nets, watch for unintended latches (incomplete if/case in RTL).
Classic RTL trap (interviewers love this) In Verilog, an incompletely-specified combinational always block (missing else or default) infers a latch, because the tool must "remember" the old value. Fix: assign a default at the top, or always cover every branch. Latches are hard to time and a verification headache.

Karnaugh / minimization (be able to do a 4-var K-map)

Group 1s in powers of two, wrap edges, larger groups = fewer literals. Know don't-cares (X) help minimization. Mention Quine–McCluskey as the tabular/automatable version.

4 · Sequential logic & timing

4.1 Latch vs flip-flop

Latch (level)Flip-flop (edge)
Triggers onClock level (transparent when enabled)Clock edge (samples once)
TimingHarder (time-borrowing, transparency)Cleaner, predictable
UseLow-power, time-borrow pathsDefault storage element in sync design

4.2 Setup & hold — the heart of timing

Setup time (tsu): data must be stable before the clock edge.
Hold time (th): data must be stable after the clock edge.

Setup check (max-delay / slow path):
  T_clk  >=  t_cq + t_comb(max) + t_su + t_skew
        → fix by slowing clock, shortening logic, pipelining

Hold check (min-delay / fast path):  INDEPENDENT of clock period
  t_cq + t_comb(min)  >=  t_h + t_skew
        → fix by adding delay/buffers; cannot fix by changing freq
Probe-then-push "Why can't you fix a hold violation by slowing the clock?" → Because hold is a same-edge race between launch and capture flops; the clock period never enters the inequality. You fix hold by adding delay to the data path (or fixing skew), not by changing frequency. Setup is the one that scales with the clock period.

4.3 Clock skew & jitter

  • Skew = spatial difference in clock arrival between flops (static). Helpful skew can aid setup but hurts hold.
  • Jitter = temporal cycle-to-cycle variation of the clock edge (PLL noise). Eats into the timing budget.
  • Clock tree synthesis (CTS) balances skew. A balanced clock tree distributes the clock with minimal skew.

4.4 FSM — Mealy vs Moore

MooreMealy
Output depends onState onlyState + current input
TimingGlitch-free, registered outputsFewer states, but output can glitch with input
LatencyOne cycle laterSame cycle (faster react)

State encoding: binary (fewest FFs), one-hot (fastest decode, easy debug, common in FPGAs), gray (one bit changes — relevant to CDC). Always know how you'd reset to a known state and handle illegal states (default → safe state).

4.5 Pipelining (switch datapaths are deep pipelines)

Break a long combinational path with registers → higher clock, higher throughput, at the cost of latency and area. Throughput = one result/clock once full; latency = depth × cycle. A packet-processing pipeline (parse → lookup → modify → queue) is exactly this.

5 · Metastability — the bridge to CDC

What it is: if a flip-flop's data changes within its setup/hold window, its output can hang at an intermediate / indeterminate voltage (neither solid 0 nor 1) for an unbounded time before resolving randomly to 0 or 1.

When it happens: whenever you sample an asynchronous signal (a signal from a different/unrelated clock) with your clock — you cannot guarantee setup/hold. This is unavoidable at a clock-domain crossing.

  • You can never make metastability impossible — only make the probability of unresolved metastability negligibly small (improve MTBF, §7).
  • The danger isn't metastability itself — it's different downstream gates sampling the half-resolved value differently (one sees 1, one sees 0) → inconsistent state.
  • Resolution time constant τ depends on the flop's gain-bandwidth; giving the signal more time (extra sync flops) lets it resolve.
This is the EE gap that twice closed Intel loops Be able to draw it: a flop, data toggling near the edge, output drawn hovering at mid-rail then snapping. Then explain "I give it a second flop a full cycle later to resolve." That visual + the MTBF formula is what separates "I've heard of it" from "I understand it."

6 · CDC fundamentals

A clock domain crossing is any signal generated in one clock domain and sampled in another. On a switch ASIC there are many: per-lane SerDes recovered clocks, MAC clock, packet-processing core clock, memory clocks, management/CPU bus clock (e.g. AXI/APB), JTAG/test clock.

6.1 Why CDC is hard

  • No fixed phase relationship → setup/hold cannot be guaranteed → metastability risk on every crossing.
  • Static timing analysis (STA) can't meaningfully time an async path — CDC must be handled structurally (synchronizers) and verified with CDC tools (e.g. Spyglass-CDC, Questa-CDC) + functional sims with metastability injection.

6.2 The three crossing types

CrossingCorrect technique
Single-bit level/control2-FF (or 3-FF) synchronizer
Single-bit pulse (fast→slow)Toggle/handshake (pulse can be missed otherwise)
Multi-bit bus / dataAsync FIFO (gray-coded pointers) OR handshake (req/ack + hold data stable) OR MUX-recirculation with a synchronized enable
Cardinal rule Never run a multi-bit bus through parallel 2-FF synchronizers. Each bit resolves independently → you can latch a value that never existed on the bus (data incoherency). Multi-bit must cross as a unit: FIFO, handshake, or gray code.

7 · Synchronizers & MTBF

7.1 The 2-flop synchronizer

// Single-bit level crossing into clk_dst domain
always @(posedge clk_dst or negedge rst_n)
  if (!rst_n) {sync2, sync1} <= 2'b0;
  else        {sync2, sync1} <= {sync1, async_in};
// sync2 is the safe, synchronized signal

FF1 may go metastable; it's given (almost) a full destination clock cycle to resolve before FF2 samples it. FF2's output is treated as clean. Use a 3rd flop when the destination clock is very fast / reliability target is high.

7.2 MTBF — quantify it (this is the EE-depth move)

           e^(t_r / τ)
MTBF  =  ───────────────────
          f_clk · f_data · T_w

t_r  = resolution time available (≈ one dst clock period minus setup)
τ    = flop resolution time constant (technology/library param)
T_w  = metastability "window" width
f_clk  = sampling clock frequency
f_data = rate of async transitions
  • MTBF grows exponentially with available resolution time t_r → that's exactly why adding a flop (more tr) helps so dramatically.
  • Faster clocks / faster data → lower MTBF (worse) → may need 3 flops.
60-sec spoken answer (rehearse) "A 2-FF synchronizer lets the first flop's metastable output resolve over almost a full destination cycle before the second flop samples it. The probability it's still unresolved drops exponentially with the resolution time available, captured by the MTBF formula — so for high-frequency or high-reliability crossings I'd add a third flop. It only works for a single bit; multi-bit buses need a FIFO or handshake so bits stay coherent."

7.3 Pulse synchronizer (fast → slow domain)

A 1-cycle pulse in a fast domain may be missed by a slow sampling clock. Solution: convert the pulse to a toggle (flip a level on each pulse), synchronize the level with 2 flops, then edge-detect (XOR of last two synced values) in the destination to regenerate the pulse.

8 · Async FIFO + gray code (multi-bit data crossing)

The standard way to move a data stream across two clocks. A dual-port RAM with a write side in clk_w and a read side in clk_r; full/empty derived by comparing pointers across domains.

8.1 Why gray-code the pointers

  • The write pointer must be read in the read domain (for empty), and vice-versa (for full) → the pointer itself is a multi-bit CDC.
  • Gray code changes only one bit per increment. So even if the synchronizer samples mid-transition, the worst case is the old value or the new value — never a wildly wrong intermediate. Binary pointers could glitch by many counts.
  • Cross each gray pointer with a 2-FF synchronizer, then compare.

8.2 Full / empty generation

FlagComputed inCondition
EmptyRead domainsynced write-ptr == read-ptr
FullWrite domainsynced read-ptr == write-ptr with MSBs inverted (wrapped one lap)
Why the extra MSB trick The FIFO pointer is log2(depth)+1 bits: the extra top bit distinguishes "full" from "empty" when read==write addresses. Empty = pointers fully equal; Full = lower bits equal but the wrap bit differs.

8.3 Binary ↔ Gray conversion (know the one-liners)

gray   = bin ^ (bin >> 1);          // binary → gray

// gray → binary (XOR-prefix)
bin = gray;
for (i = 1; i < N; i++) bin ^= (gray >> i);

9 · CDC bug classes (verification target)

As a pre-si validation engineer, you're hunting these. Be able to name them, explain the mechanism, and say how you'd catch each.

10 · Reset architecture & RDC

10.1 Async vs sync reset

Async resetSync reset
AssertImmediate, no clock neededOn clock edge only
RiskReset removal near clock edge → metastabilityNeeds running clock; widens data path logic

Best practice — "asynchronous assert, synchronous de-assert" via a reset synchronizer: assert immediately, but release the reset synchronized to the clock so all flops leave reset on the same edge (no metastability on reset recovery).

10.2 RDC — Reset Domain Crossing

The reset analogue of CDC: a flop in one reset domain drives a flop in a different reset domain. If the source resets while the destination doesn't (or vice-versa), the destination can capture a metastable/garbage value. Modern switch SoCs with many independent power/reset domains must RDC-verify, not just CDC-verify.

11 · Ethernet fundamentals

The switch ASIC's whole job is to move Ethernet frames. You don't need to be a protocol engineer, but you must be able to place the switch in the networking stack and describe what each layer does to a packet. This is the vocabulary that makes your validation answers sound credible.

The one-liner that places the chip "A classic switch is an L2 device — it forwards Ethernet frames by destination MAC. A modern datacenter switch (Tomahawk/Trident) is L2+L3: it also routes IP packets, does VLANs, ACLs, ECMP, and QoS. Physically it's a pile of SerDes lanes → PHY → MAC → packet pipeline → buffers/scheduler → back out."

11.2 The Ethernet frame — know every field

┌──────────┬─────┬──────────┬──────────┬───────┬───────────┬──────────┬─────┐
│ Preamble │ SFD │ Dst MAC  │ Src MAC  │ Type/ │  Payload  │   FCS    │ IFG │
│  7 B     │ 1 B │   6 B    │   6 B    │ Len 2 │ 46–1500 B │  4 B CRC │12 B │
└──────────┴─────┴──────────┴──────────┴───────┴───────────┴──────────┴─────┘
        (+ optional 802.1Q VLAN tag, 4 B, after Src MAC)
  • Preamble + SFD (Start Frame Delimiter): 1010… clock-sync pattern; SFD 10101011 marks the byte boundary where the frame proper begins.
  • Destination MAC (6 B): who the frame is for. First bit = unicast(0)/multicast(1); broadcast = all-ones FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF. This is the L2 lookup key.
  • Source MAC (6 B): who sent it — the switch learns (src MAC → ingress port) to build its MAC table.
  • 802.1Q VLAN tag (optional 4 B): TPID 0x8100 + VLAN ID (12 bits) + priority (3 bits, PCP for QoS). Lets one wire carry many isolated L2 domains.
  • EtherType / Length: ≥0x0600 = type (e.g. 0x0800 IPv4, 0x86DD IPv6, 0x0806 ARP); else it's a length.
  • Payload (46–1500 B): the upper-layer packet; padded to 46 B minimum. Jumbo frames push to ~9000 B.
  • FCS (4 B CRC-32): integrity check over the whole frame; bad FCS → drop + error counter (a key validation check).
  • IFG (Inter-Frame Gap, 12 B): mandatory idle between frames so receivers can re-sync.
Why min frame = 64 B (collision-era fact still asked) On legacy half-duplex CSMA/CD, a sender had to still be transmitting when the farthest collision could come back — the slot time. 64 B is the minimum that guarantees collision detection across the max cable length. Modern full-duplex switched links have no collisions, but the 64 B minimum survives for compatibility (hence the 46 B payload floor).

MII family (the MAC↔PHY interface): MII → GMII (1 G) → XGMII (10 G) → XLGMII/CGMII (40/100 G). At high speed the actual interface is internal (the PHY is on-die). Naming pattern worth recognizing, not memorizing.

11.4 Speeds & encoding (recognize, don't cram)

Why FEC appears at high rate (ties to PAM4) PAM4 packs 2 bits/symbol (4 voltage levels) to double rate at the same baud, but the eye is 1/3 the height → far higher bit-error rate. Forward Error Correction (Reed-Solomon) corrects errors in-line so the link is usable without constant retransmits. Same reasoning as PCIe Gen6.

11.5 Flow control & congestion (switch-specific, likely probed)

  • 802.3x PAUSE: link-level "stop sending" frame — crude, pauses everything.
  • 802.1Qbb PFC (Priority Flow Control): per-priority pause — lets you make one traffic class lossless (used for RDMA/RoCE) without stalling others.
  • ECN (Explicit Congestion Notification): mark, don't drop — the switch sets a bit so endpoints slow down before buffers overflow.
  • Head-of-line blocking / buffer sharing: why switches use VOQs (virtual output queues) and shared-buffer MMUs — a validation coverage axis.

12.1 The crossings hidden in that path (the CDC map)

Every ── CDC ── above is a validation target. A frame crosses 4+ clock domains one way: lane → PHY → MAC → pipeline, then back out. Multi-bit data crossings use async FIFOs (§8); control/status (link-up, pause, credit) use 2-FF synchronizers (§7). This is exactly why Broadcom asked for CDC depth — the datapath is a chain of clock-domain crossings.

15.3 Enumeration & config space (how the system finds a device)

  1. Topology is a tree: Root Complex → (Switches: up/down ports) → Endpoints. Each link is point-to-point.
  2. Config space: each function has 4 KB (PCIe extended); first 256 B is PCI-compatible. Header has Vendor/Device ID, BARs, capability pointers.
  3. Enumeration (BIOS/OS): depth-first walk of buses; read Vendor ID at each (Bus,Dev,Fn) — 0xFFFF = no device. Assign bus numbers across bridges.
  4. BAR sizing: write all-1s to a BAR, read back to learn the size/alignment, then assign a non-overlapping address window. Program MMIO/IO space.
  5. Capabilities: walk the capability linked list — MSI/MSI-X, PCIe cap (link/slot), Power Management, AER, etc.

15.4 Interrupts — MSI / MSI-X (why no INTx on modern parts)

  • MSI/MSI-X = a memory write to a special address with a data payload; it travels as a posted TLP in-band — no dedicated interrupt pin, no sideband.
  • MSI-X scales to many vectors (a table in device memory), each with its own address/data → per-queue interrupts (exactly what NICs/switches and NVMe use).
  • Validation angle: interrupt fires once per event, vector maps to the right handler, masking/pending bits behave, and ordering — the interrupt's posted write must not pass the data it signals (producer/consumer ordering).

15.5 Link power states & the ordering rules (high-value detail)

StateMeaning
L0Fully active.
L0sLow-exit-latency idle on one direction; fast wake.
L1Deeper; link powered but quiescent. ASPM L1 negotiates it; L1.1/L1.2 add substates (CLKREQ-based) for more savings. (My Zebu work touched the entry/exit handshake; I do NOT claim real L1.2 electricals.)
L2/L3Aux-powered / off — device essentially asleep; wake via WAKE#/PME.
TLP ordering — the one rule to remember Posted writes (MWr) push non-posted reads — a read completion can't arrive before a previously-issued write to the same path takes effect. This producer/consumer ordering is why a device writes data then writes the "done" flag/MSI, and the system sees the data first. Most data-corruption-under-stress bugs trace to an ordering or flow-control-credit violation here.

  • The control/management plane of a switch ASIC is often reached over PCIe (host configures tables/counters) — config space + BAR + MSI knowledge applies directly.
  • Reliable-delivery + retry-buffer thinking maps onto FIFO/backpressure verification (§13).

Tier A — bit-ops & hardware-adjacent C (most likely)

Q1 · Count set bits / check power of two

Count 1-bits, and test if a number is a power of two.

int popcount(uint32_t x){
    int c = 0;
    while (x) { x &= (x - 1); c++; }   // clears lowest set bit each pass
    return c;
}
int is_pow2(uint32_t x){
    return x && !(x & (x - 1));        // exactly one set bit, x != 0
}

edge cases x==0 → popcount 0, is_pow2 false. Mention __builtin_popcount exists.

Q2 · Reverse the bits of a 32-bit word 
uint32_t revbits(uint32_t x){
    uint32_t r = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < 32; i++){
        r = (r << 1) | (x & 1);
        x >>= 1;
    }
    return r;
}

why hardware cares endianness / bus-bit-ordering between blocks.

Q3 · Combine two 32-bit register reads into a 64-bit value (Qualcomm asked this) 
uint64_t make64(uint32_t hi, uint32_t lo){
    return ((uint64_t)hi << 32) | (uint64_t)lo;
}
// TRAP: (hi << 32) on a 32-bit type is undefined — widen BEFORE shifting.

narrate "Because the registers are 32-bit, I cast to 64-bit before the shift, and preserve hi/lo ordering."

Q4 · MMIO register read-modify-write with volatile 
#define REG(addr) (*(volatile uint32_t *)(addr))

void set_field(uintptr_t addr, uint32_t mask, uint32_t val){
    uint32_t r = REG(addr);          // read
    r &= ~mask;                       // clear field
    r |= (val & mask);                // set field
    REG(addr) = r;                    // write back
}

why volatile stops the compiler caching/reordering the MMIO access. Mention memory barriers for ordering across devices.

Q5 · Generate cache-line-boundary-crossing write addresses (Qualcomm cacheline probe)

void gen_overlap(uintptr_t base, uintptr_t end, int line, int wr){

        uintptr_t off = a + (line - wr/2);  // start near end of line
        // write of size `wr` at `off` spills into next line

}

Q6 · Binary ↔ Gray code (CDC pointer core skill) 
uint32_t bin2gray(uint32_t b){ return b ^ (b >> 1); }

    uint32_t b = g;
    while (g >>= 1) b ^= g;   // XOR-prefix

}

Q7 · Circular (ring) FIFO — full/empty, the async-FIFO logic in C 
typedef struct {
    uint32_t buf[SIZE];     // SIZE a power of two
    uint32_t wr, rd;        // free-running, use extra MSB for full/empty
} fifo_t;

int is_empty(fifo_t *f){ return f->wr == f->rd; }
int is_full (fifo_t *f){ return (f->wr ^ f->rd) == SIZE; } // MSB differs, low bits equal

int push(fifo_t *f, uint32_t v){
    if (is_full(f)) return -1;
    f->buf[f->wr & (SIZE-1)] = v;
    f->wr++;
    return 0;
}
int pop(fifo_t *f, uint32_t *v){
    if (is_empty(f)) return -1;
    *v = f->buf[f->rd & (SIZE-1)];
    f->rd++;
    return 0;
}

edge cases wrap-around, full vs empty disambiguation via the extra pointer bit. This is the async-FIFO concept (§8).

Q8 · Moving average via circular buffer (Cerebras — watch the wrap) 
typedef struct { int buf[N]; int idx, cnt; long sum; } mavg_t;

double update(mavg_t *m, int x){
    m->sum -= m->buf[m->idx];      // subtract oldest
    m->buf[m->idx] = x;            // overwrite
    m->sum += x;                   // add newest
    m->idx = (m->idx + 1) % N;     // wrap
    if (m->cnt < N) m->cnt++;
    return (double)m->sum / m->cnt; // divide by count, not N, while filling
}

bug I made dividing by N before the window fills. Handle the fill phase.

Q10 · FSM in C — simple sequence/edge detector 
// Detect a rising edge on a sampled async input after 2-FF sync
typedef enum { LOW, HIGH } st_t;
int rising_edge(st_t *s, int sync_in){

Q14 · Find the missing number in 0..n (XOR trick) 
int missing(int *a, int n){      // a has n items from 0..n, one missing
    int x = n;                    // start with the top value
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) x ^= i ^ a[i];
    return x;                     // pairs cancel, missing survives
}

Q15 · Move all zeros to the end, keep order (in place)

}

Q16 · Two-sum on a sorted array (two pointers, O(n)) 
int two_sum_sorted(int *a, int n, int target, int *i_out, int *j_out){
    int i = 0, j = n - 1;
    while (i < j) {
        int s = a[i] + a[j];
        if (s == target) { *i_out = i; *j_out = j; return 1; }
        if (s < target) i++; else j--;
    }
    return 0;                          // no pair
}

D1 · CDC — the full decision flow (not a short description)

The gap with high-level notes is they tell you what a synchronizer is but not how an engineer decides which one to use and how a tool proves it. Here is the complete flow, the way you'd reason on the job.

D1.1 Decision tree — "I have a signal crossing a clock boundary"

START: signal X generated in clk_A, sampled in clk_B
  │
  ├─ Is X a single bit?
  │     ├─ YES → Is it a level (stays set) or a pulse (1 cycle)?
  │     │         ├─ LEVEL  → 2-FF synchronizer (3-FF if clk_B very fast)
  │     │         └─ PULSE  → fast→slow? toggle-synchronizer + edge-detect
  │     │                     slow→fast? 2-FF may be ok if pulse ≥ 1 dst cycle
  │     └─ NO (multi-bit) ↓
  │
  ├─ Is X a data BUS that changes every cycle (a stream)?
  │     └─ YES → ASYNC FIFO (gray-coded pointers)  [§8]
  │
  ├─ Is X a multi-bit value that changes occasionally (config/status)?
  │     ├─ Can the source hold it stable until ack? → REQ/ACK HANDSHAKE
  │     │     (sync the req with 2-FF, hold data stable, sync ack back)
  │     └─ Is it a counter/pointer only? → GRAY-CODE it, then 2-FF
  │
  └─ Are there MULTIPLE related signals that must stay aligned?
        └─ YES → cross them as ONE unit (FIFO/handshake), never independently
                 (independent crossing = RECONVERGENCE bug)

D1.2 The handshake crossing in full (the one people hand-wave)

Source domain (clk_A)            Destination domain (clk_B)
─────────────────────            ──────────────────────────
1. drive DATA bus (stable)
2. assert REQ           ───2FF──► 3. REQ_sync seen
                                  4. capture DATA (now stable, safe)
                                  5. assert ACK
6. ACK_sync seen   ◄───2FF─────
7. deassert REQ, may change DATA
                                  8. deassert ACK
  • Why DATA is safe without synchronizing each bit: it is held stable by the source until ACK returns, so when the destination captures it (step 4) it is not mid-transition. Only the 1-bit REQ/ACK need synchronizers.
  • Cost: a full round-trip of synchronizer latency per transfer → low throughput. That's why streams use FIFOs instead.
  • The bug if you skip "hold stable": source changes DATA before ACK → destination latches torn/partial data. This is the data-hold CDC bug.

D1.3 How a CDC tool actually proves it (so you can speak to verification)

  1. Structural pass (no simulation): the tool builds the clock-domain graph from the RTL + a clock constraint file, finds every signal where launch-clock ≠ capture-clock, and checks each has a recognized synchronizer structure. Output: list of unsynchronized or unsafe crossings. This is the first thing run on new RTL.
  2. Protocol/structural checks: multi-bit bus through parallel FFs → flagged as data-incoherency risk; combinational logic before a synchronizer → glitch risk; two synced copies of one source recombining → reconvergence.
  3. Metastability injection (simulation): the tool/model randomly delays or inverts the first synchronizer flop's output by a cycle to emulate real metastable resolution, then runs functional tests. If downstream logic mis-behaves, the crossing isn't robust to metastability even though it "passed structurally."
  4. Assertions: SVA properties — "REQ must remain high until ACK," "DATA stable while REQ asserted," "every req eventually gets an ack" — catch protocol violations dynamically.
  5. Formal: proves the handshake FSM can't deadlock for any clock ratio.
Full-flow answer (90 sec) "When I hit a crossing I first ask: one bit or many? A single level bit gets a 2-FF synchronizer; a pulse gets a toggle-and-edge-detect so it isn't missed. A continuous data bus crosses through an async FIFO with gray-coded pointers, because gray code guarantees only one pointer bit changes per step. An occasional multi-bit value crosses with a req/ack handshake where the source holds the data stable until the ack returns, so only the 1-bit req and ack need synchronizing. On the verification side, a structural CDC tool finds every crossing and checks it has a synchronizer, metastability injection in simulation models the real half-resolved value to prove downstream logic is robust, and SVA plus formal prove the handshake can't deadlock or tear data. The cardinal rule is multi-bit signals cross as a unit — never through independent synchronizers — or you get data incoherency."

D2 · Timing closure — the full flow behind setup/hold

High-level notes give you the two inequalities. Here's the full chain from a path to a fix, so a follow-up "and then what?" doesn't run out of road.

D2.1 The complete path budget

     launch FF                combinational logic              capture FF
 clk→[FF]──Q──►[ logic: gates + wires, delay t_comb ]──►D──[FF]
        │                                                      │
   clk arrives                                            clk arrives
   at T=0+t_skew_launch                                   at T=Tclk+t_skew_capture

SETUP (can the data arrive in time for the NEXT edge?):
   t_cq + t_comb(max) + t_setup  ≤  T_clk + (t_skew_capture − t_skew_launch) − t_jitter
   slack_setup = RHS − LHS    (must be ≥ 0)

HOLD (does new data NOT corrupt the SAME edge's capture?):
   t_cq + t_comb(min)  ≥  t_hold + (t_skew_capture − t_skew_launch)
   slack_hold = LHS − RHS     (must be ≥ 0)

D2.2 Why each term is there (the part that gets probed)

  • t_cq (clock-to-Q): launch flop's own delay to put data on Q after its edge.
  • t_comb: the logic between flops. Max path drives setup; min (shortest) path drives hold. A single register pair has both a worst and best case path.
  • t_setup / t_hold: the capture flop's requirement window.
  • skew (capture − launch): if the capture clock arrives later (positive skew), it helps setup (more time) but hurts hold (the new data races in while the old edge is still effectively "open"). This is why fixing skew to help setup can create hold violations.
  • jitter: cycle-to-cycle clock edge uncertainty (PLL noise); eats setup margin, modeled as a guard band.

D2.3 The fix ladder (what you do when slack is negative)

ViolationFix, in order tried
Setup (negative)1) shorten logic / restructure 2) pipeline (add a register stage) 3) upsize/buffer slow gates 4) better placement (shorter wires) 5) useful-skew (delay capture clock) 6) last resort: lower frequency
Hold (negative)1) add delay buffers on the short data path 2) fix clock skew/balance the tree 3) downsize over-fast gates. Never change frequency — period isn't in the hold equation.

D2.4 Why this matters for a switch ASIC validator

You won't close timing (that's PD/STA), but you must recognize timing-rooted symptoms: a block that works in slow simulation but fails at-speed on emulation/silicon, intermittent data corruption that tracks temperature/voltage (PVT corner), or a CDC path that "passes" structurally but tears data under metastability. Being able to say "that smells like a hold/skew issue at the domain boundary, let's check the synchronizer and the constraint" is the credible-validator move.
